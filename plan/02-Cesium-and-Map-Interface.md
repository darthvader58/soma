# Part 2: Cesium and the Map Interface

## The map is how the user understands the plan

Cesium should make SOMA's answer visible, not become a second analysis engine. A user should be able to choose a surface location, recognize the surrounding terrain, watch a relay move into view, and understand why a transfer starts or stops. The primary action should be “Plan delivery,” and the primary result should be a route with a timed sequence of transfers and waits. Browsing terrain and comparing sites remain supporting interactions. NASA's published Moon Trek work provides precedent for bringing lunar data and analysis into an interactive web portal, but it does not establish that SOMA's interface will outperform existing tools or that Cesium is scientifically superior to another renderer. Those would require separate user or engineering evaluations. ([Day and Law, P14](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p14))

Choosing Cesium is a proposed implementation decision supported by its current Moon dataset and time-dynamic visualization APIs. The research evidence belongs to the quantities displayed: lunar geometry from the SPICE literature, terrain horizons from the illumination papers, and scheduled transfers from the communications literature. This distinction prevents an attractive globe from becoming accidental evidence for a scientific claim. A green line on a rendered scene means nothing unless the backend has supplied a contact record that passed the declared checks. ([P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [P03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p03), [P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11), [D05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d05))

## Start with the current official Moon setup

The current Cesium Moon product is a global **3D Tiles** dataset that combines imagery and terrain. The official CesiumJS example sets the default ellipsoid to the Moon, creates a viewer with the ordinary globe disabled, disables the Earth imagery picker and geocoder, and loads ion asset **2684829** as a Cesium3DTileset. The documented example also disables the scene-mode picker because the global-tileset configuration does not support the usual 2D and Columbus View modes. SOMA should begin with that documented configuration rather than transplant an Earth viewer and assume a different texture makes it lunar. ([D05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d05))

The phrase “Moon terrain” can hide an important API distinction. The official asset is loaded as a tileset in the scene, not as the usual TerrainProvider underneath a Globe. A Cesium3DTilesTerrainProvider exists in the current documentation, but it is labeled experimental, and the official Moon example does not establish that loading this asset through that provider is the supported baseline. Therefore the first implementation should use the documented tileset workflow and avoid promising that sampleTerrainMostDetailed will operate on the Moon asset. The fact that two APIs both concern terrain does not make them interchangeable. ([D05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d05), [D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11))

Cesium publishes different approximate source resolutions for different regions. Its mid-latitude terrain is approximately 59 meters, while the polar sources become finer toward the poles, reaching approximately five-meter terrain in the innermost listed polar coverage. The presence of approximately one-meter polar imagery does not mean the terrain elevations are one-meter measurements. Nor does a source resolution mean every currently streamed mesh triangle or picked location has that fidelity. SOMA should show the visual-source attribution while separately showing the numerical DEM and quality assumptions used in the analysis. ([D05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d05))

## Choosing a location without confusing display and analysis

In the documented globe-disabled scene, the natural click-to-place mechanism is the depth-buffer position returned by scene.pickPosition, after checking platform support and whether a position was actually returned. Clicking the sky, an orbit marker, or an unloaded portion of the scene must not silently create a lander. The UI should distinguish selection of a surface point from selection of an asset, and should retain the original pick as a visual interaction rather than declaring its elevation authoritative. A coordinate-entry fallback is important for users whose browser or device cannot support the needed depth operations. ([D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11))

After a surface click, the frontend should send the chosen lunar coordinates to the Python service. The service resolves the position against its declared DEM, checks coverage, identifies the reference frame and elevation datum, and returns the analysis location. If the numerical surface and rendered mesh differ, show a small explanation instead of silently moving the physical model to match the graphics. The lander marker can remain visually legible with a display offset, but that offset must not become extra antenna height in the calculation. The interface should show the actual numerical coordinates and both configured equipment heights in its scenario details. ([P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05), [D04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d04))

Cesium's scene-level sampleHeightMostDetailed and clampToHeightMostDetailed functions can query rendered 3D Tiles at available detail, subject to their documented 3D and depth-texture requirements. They can be useful for visual placement or diagnostics and may return no usable result. They should not replace the scientific terrain pipeline. Reproducible horizon analysis requires a pinned numerical dataset, defined sampling, and explicit missing-data handling, not a result that changes with what happened to be rendered. ([D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11), [P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05))

## Keep every displayed asset in a declared lunar frame

The safest first release should export sampled Cartesian positions already transformed into the chosen Moon-fixed display frame. A lander remains fixed in that frame while relay samples move over time. Cesium can interpolate those samples for smooth animation, but the backend's more carefully sampled state and contact histories remain authoritative. Positions should be in meters, timestamps should be explicit, and interpolation must stop at the data-coverage boundary. An orbit line extended beyond its ephemeris interval is not a prediction merely because the renderer can draw it. ([P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11))

CZML's position reference-frame labels include FIXED and INERTIAL, and sampled-position APIs default to FIXED. In a lunar application, the word FIXED must be paired with a documented scene convention; it is not a universal identifier for the particular SPICE mean-Earth frame used by the DEM. Backend conversion and landmark registration must establish that convention. Require a versioned transform from the selected SPICE/DEM frame into the renderer frame used by the loaded tileset, even if the documented transform is the identity. Derive it from source metadata and frame definitions wherever available, then verify it against several well-distributed lunar landmarks rather than fitting an arbitrary visual shift. Declare a regional registration tolerance justified by source accuracy and intended display scale, record the residuals, and block release of the map/contact visualization if the checks exceed that tolerance. A generic CZML FIXED label is not the frame identity, and a display alignment must never rewrite the numerical terrain model. Cesium also exposes Moon-fixed celestial transformations, but its normal Earth-oriented transformations should not be assumed correct simply because the ellipsoid changed. The baseline avoids that ambiguity by supplying Moon-fixed samples. A later inertial-view feature should be added only after its central-body transform is explicitly configured and cross-checked against the backend. ([D03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d03), [D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11))

Earth should appear as a correctly positioned distant target or as a simplified directional marker, with the selected ground station named in the transfer details. If the first interface uses a diagrammatic Earth marker so the route fits on screen, label it as not to scale. Do not calculate Earth-station coordinates with the Moon ellipsoid merely because it is the viewer's default. Similarly, enlarging a tiny satellite model for legibility must not affect range, occlusion, or antenna pointing. Physical state, rendered position, and icon scale should be separate fields.

## Show sunlight without letting the renderer determine it

Changing a globe's ellipsoid does not establish a validated lunar Sun or shadow model. Cesium's planetary-position APIs document Earth-centered inertial outputs, and the existence of SunLight or scene shadows does not guarantee that the selected lunar frame, time, and topography are handled in the same way as the analysis. The scientific Sun direction and solar-visible fraction should therefore come from the Python model. If the scene is illuminated for realism, use a deliberately configured light direction and validate it against those values. If that cannot be made reliable in the first release, use neutral lighting and display the computed Sun arrow and horizon information instead. A clearly labeled schematic is better than persuasive but incorrect shading. ([D11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d11), [P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05))

An optional site-level horizon inset should plot the computed terrain horizon and the current Sun, Earth, and relay directions. Its value is explanatory: it can show that Earth is above the ideal horizontal but below a local crater rim, or that a small part of the Sun remains visible. The inset should distinguish an Earth-center direction from an actual ground-station line of sight. It should also identify whether it shows the antenna height or panel height. This interaction directly exposes the quantities used by the horizon-method literature instead of asking the user to infer them from a cinematic camera angle. ([P03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p03), [P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05), [D04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d04))

## The schedule needs a timeline and a readable explanation

The map and timeline should share one selected time. Moving the time control should update the relay position, the currently active contact, the lander's stored energy, the data already delivered, and the data waiting on each modeled node. It should not rerun an expensive optimization for every animation frame. A new scenario or a new planning horizon starts a new analysis job; scrubbing through an existing result reads the same immutable result. Transfer intervals should identify sender, receiver, usable rate, allocated data, and the reason the interval ends. A storage interval should say where the data waits and which next contact it is waiting for. These are proposed interface behaviors derived from the contact-plan model. ([P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11), [P12](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p12))

Available contacts and selected transmissions must look different. An outlined or dashed line can represent a feasible opportunity, while a solid emphasized line represents a transfer actually allocated by the scheduler. A line should disappear or change status when the relevant interval ends. Do not color every geometric line green, and do not hide blocked opportunities without an explanation. Text labels should distinguish terrain blockage, body occultation, insufficient link margin, missing service allocation, radio conflict, inadequate battery reserve, and unknown input coverage. Color alone is insufficient for accessibility or technical clarity.

The completed result should begin with a short sentence stating the modeled outcome, followed by the concrete schedule. For example, the application could explain that the file waits on a relay until its next Earth contact, or that direct-to-Earth cannot fit the full volume before the deadline. Such sentences must be generated from structured result fields rather than invented by a language model. The interface should offer a second site or date as a controlled alternative, not overwhelm the user with an atlas of unrelated metrics. This presentation is SOMA's proposed product design; no usability improvement is claimed until it is tested.

## Authentication, reliability, and the interface completion gate

Using Cesium Moon in a custom application requires an ion account and appropriate asset access. A production-facing app should use a scoped application token and the required attributions, not the bundled evaluation token. Pricing, quotas, and acceptable uses depend on the actual account and terms; the plan does not assume free unlimited streaming or permission to redistribute ion-hosted tiles. A fallback neutral Moon or local numerical report can keep the planning result readable if the visual service fails, but it must be labeled as a fallback rather than passed off as detailed terrain. ([D12](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d12))

The interface is ready only when a selected point matches the backend's declared location, an asset at a selected timestamp matches the exported state, the timeline matches the actual schedule, and missing terrain or ephemeris data produces an explicit warning. Verify that local and distant camera views do not change any computed result. Test a no-depth-support device, a failed tile request, a stale analysis response, and an unsupported location. These checks validate the contract between rendering and analysis; they do not replace the scientific checks in the other chapters.
