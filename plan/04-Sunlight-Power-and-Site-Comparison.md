# Part 4: Sunlight, Power, and Landing-Site Comparison

## Why sunlight belongs in a communications planner

A communications window is useful only if the lander can afford to use it. Transmitting consumes electrical energy, the vehicle has other loads, and its solar array may be illuminated at a time when no receiving asset is available. Conversely, a relay can appear while the array is shadowed, in which case the battery may make transmission possible. SOMA should therefore join lighting, power, and communications along the same timeline rather than requiring them to occur simultaneously. A battery is precisely what allows energy collected earlier to support an operation later. This reasoning is reflected in the LuSEE-Night power-design study, which connects simulated generation, consumption, battery reserves, and the mission's concept of operations. Its particular hardware and nonpolar site must not be copied as universal lunar defaults. ([Saliwanchik et al., P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

The Sun is not included merely to satisfy a challenge checkbox. It is an input to the decision that SOMA promises to make. If the communications schedule fails because the lander would cross its reserve threshold, the user should be able to see the shadow interval or unfavorable array orientation responsible. If moving the date creates a feasible schedule, the application should identify whether the improvement came from more energy, a better relay opportunity, a different Earth-station window, or some combination. This explanation is a proposed SOMA feature grounded in the separation of illumination analysis and resource-constrained scheduling, not a published result for the proposed application. ([P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05), [P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06), [P13](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p13))

## The terrain-horizon method

Imagine standing at the lander's antenna or solar-array height and drawing the skyline through a complete turn. In each direction, a ridge or crater rim may sit above the mathematical horizontal. The Sun can be above the ideal spherical horizon but still hidden behind that terrain. Mazarico and colleagues describe computing horizon elevations in many directions from LOLA topography, then comparing celestial geometry with those profiles. Their modeling was compared with LRO Wide-Angle Camera imagery. Gläser and colleagues apply higher-resolution terrain and study how conditions change with observer height. This is the research basis for SOMA's reusable horizon profiles. It is not an assumption that any bright-looking map pixel is continuously sunlit. ([P03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p03), [P04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p04))

For each terrain sample, the implementation should form the three-dimensional vector from the observer to that sample in the declared Moon-fixed frame, then resolve it into local horizontal and vertical components. The elevation angle follows from those components. The highest blocking angle at each azimuth defines the horizon profile. Doing this in body-consistent Cartesian geometry includes the curvature of the reference surface instead of importing an Earth-specific correction. Nearby details need finer sampling because a small obstacle close to an antenna can occupy a large angle. Distant terrain can still establish the skyline, so the method needs a documented far-field treatment rather than stopping at the edge of the high-resolution tile. Barker and colleagues' illumination analysis explicitly separates near-field and far-field horizon contributions. ([P05, Section 5](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05))

The profile should be computed separately at the solar-array reference height and the antenna reference height. Increasing either height can change a visibility window, but a higher communications antenna does not automatically raise the panels. At the exact geographic pole, conventional north/east directions need a declared reference meridian; the numerical implementation must avoid an undefined local basis. No atmospheric-refraction correction should be imported from terrestrial sunrise or radio-horizon calculators. The point is to calculate what the specified lunar equipment can see, not what a person at Earth sea level would see. ([P04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p04), [D01](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d01))

## The Sun has a disk, not just a center point

A center-point test is useful as a coarse diagnostic but is too crude to represent partial sunrise behind a crater rim. Barker and colleagues calculate the visible fraction of the solar disk using a horizon-based method; the LuSEE-Night power paper also accounts for the fraction of the Sun below the horizon around sunrise and sunset. SOMA should therefore retain a solar-visible fraction between zero and one. A straightforward proposed implementation samples the apparent disk, tests each sample against the horizon, and refines the sampling until the visible fraction is stable. A simpler disk-segment calculation can be used when its locally flat-horizon approximation is explicitly identified. Those are numerical implementation choices whose convergence still needs testing. ([P05, Section 5](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05), [P06, Section 3](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

The geometry layer should also consider Earth occulting the Sun during a lunar eclipse if the analysis interval includes one. Terrain shadow and Earth occultation must be combined as visibility masks on the same solar disk, not naively multiplied if the blocked portions overlap. The first release may reject unsupported eclipse intervals rather than silently calculate full illumination. Reflected Earthlight, terrain-scattered light, thermal radiation, and a detailed temperature solution should remain outside the initial electrical-power model. The original illumination literature can treat richer effects, but adopting its direct-sunlight method does not mean SOMA implements every effect in that literature. ([P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [P03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p03))

## Turning sunlight into electrical power

“Sun visible” and “panel producing its rated power” are different statements. A panel receives less direct sunlight when the incoming rays are nearly parallel to its surface. In a simple engineering model, instantaneous generated power is the solar irradiance at the Moon multiplied by active panel area, electrical conversion efficiency, the nonnegative cosine of the angle between the panel normal and the Sun direction, the visible solar fraction, and declared derating factors. For several differently oriented panels, calculate each contribution separately and add them. Saliwanchik and colleagues use projected panel area, solar-disk visibility, temperature-dependent efficiency, dust and radiation derating, and structural shadowing in their lunar-instrument simulations. That paper is a concrete research basis for a time-resolved power model rather than an assumption that illumination percentage equals electrical energy. ([P06, Section 3](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

SOMA's initial model should intentionally be simpler than that hardware-specific study. Use a fixed panel configuration, an explicit normal or orientation policy, a stated efficiency, and stated lumped derating. Evaluate solar irradiance using the selected reference value and Sun distance, rather than mixing an arbitrary Earth day/night cycle into the calculation. If temperature and lander self-shadowing are not modeled, say so in the result. The derating factors are scenario assumptions, not validated uncertainty distributions. A five-percent degradation assumption from one mission paper is not a universal material property. The same applies to its panel geometry, battery size, and predicted operating schedule. ([P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

The orientation choice needs to be understandable. A horizontal panel, a fixed tilted panel, and a Sun-tracking panel are different systems. A tracker should not appear for free because the software knows where the Sun is. If selected, its mechanical limits, tracking assumptions, and power overhead must belong to a distinct hardware preset. At polar sites, the difference between a low Sun angle and a favorable panel normal is especially important. The first release can use one fixed-array preset and expose the normal in the scenario details instead of turning the application into a solar-array design optimizer.

## Battery state must constrain the schedule

The battery calculation should track stored energy through time. During a surplus, charging increases energy after charging efficiency and charge-power limits are applied. During a deficit, the difference between demand and generation removes energy after discharge efficiency is applied. Stored energy cannot exceed capacity, so excess generation is curtailed rather than accumulated in an imaginary unlimited battery. It must not fall below the configured reserve, and the battery must be capable of delivering the instantaneous discharge power demanded by a transmission. Energy capacity and power capability are separate constraints: a battery can contain enough watt-hours while being unable to supply a short high-power load. The use of a time-varying generation and consumption budget is research-backed; the precise battery model and limits remain hardware-specific inputs. ([P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

Define the battery-state convention explicitly: stored energy at the start of a bin becomes stored energy at its end after integrating generation, loads, and radio activity over that bin. Apply charging efficiency to energy entering storage and discharge efficiency to the energy removed to serve a bus-side deficit. The optimization must prevent simultaneous positive battery charge and discharge within the same bin, using a binary mode or an equivalent signed-net-power formulation with the correct efficiency branch. Surplus generation supplies loads first, then charges storage within its limits, with the remainder curtailed. A bin containing a genuine change between charging and discharging should be split rather than represented as both modes at once. Check reserve and instantaneous power within changing intervals through finer replay or conservative bounds, not only at the final timestamp.

The demand model should include a nonzero base load and incremental radio electrical loads for transmit, receive, and acquisition where applicable. Radiated RF power is not the same as the electrical power drawn from the battery. Amplifier efficiency and electronics overhead separate them. If a profile specifies a transmit mode as drawing 20 watts electrically, that must not be interpreted as an antenna radiating 20 watts unless the profile explicitly says both are equal. Receiver sensitivity belongs in the link budget, while radio electrical consumption belongs in the energy model. A schedule combines the two, but they should not share an ambiguous field named simply “power.” ([P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06), [D09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d09))

The scheduler must enforce the battery constraints while selecting transmissions, not calculate a route first and paint the resulting infeasible battery trace red afterward. A useful small-system formulation places generated energy, base demand, radio activity, charge/discharge limits, and reserve constraints on the same discretized timeline as communication opportunities. Research on time-expanded space-information networks supports coupling resource use to temporal flows, while the lunar power study supports constructing the generation and demand inputs. Combining those two pieces for SOMA is a proposed adaptation, not an already published or verified lunar algorithm. ([P12](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p12), [P13](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p13), [P06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p06))

## Terrain quality and uncertainty must be visible

The proposed initial numerical dataset is the NASA PGDA south-pole LOLA mosaic, whose product page identifies five-meter pixels, polar stereographic coordinates, and a DE421 mean-Earth frame. That does not mean the entire Moon has five-meter measured terrain, or that every five-meter pixel contains an independent laser return. The product includes interpolated elevations and provides count information. The analysis should retain coverage and quality masks, use coarser data for distant terrain where appropriate, and refuse to label an unobserved or unsupported horizon as clear. The visual Cesium dataset and numerical DEM are related sources, not automatically identical meshes or elevations. ([D04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d04), [D05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d05))

Barker and colleagues distinguish very small adjusted track geolocation errors from the larger uncertainty in the derived terrain surface. Their reported surface-height and slope uncertainties are dataset- and region-specific; they are not a blanket guarantee for all LOLA products, much less for every rendered Cesium tile. Their use of perturbed terrain realizations shows how elevation uncertainty propagates into horizon and illumination uncertainty. SOMA should initially use conservative horizon margins and clearly labeled sensitivity runs. A later ensemble mode can be justified by that research, but it should not display probability-of-success percentages without a defensible error distribution and dependency model. ([P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05))

## Comparing sites without becoming a comparison dashboard

The comparison should hold the communications task and hardware constant. If the question concerns alternative locations, keep data volume, data-ready time, deadline, initial battery state, and network assumptions unchanged. If the question concerns alternative landing dates, define whether the deadline moves with the same mission-age interval or remains an absolute Earth deadline, then keep that rule consistent. Otherwise one scenario may appear better merely because it was given more time or a fuller battery. SOMA should compare the resulting schedules, not collapse all properties into an unexplained “best landing site” score.

The main comparison outcome should be whether the dataset can be delivered within the modeled constraints. Supporting quantities are the chosen route, delivery time, lander radio energy, minimum battery reserve, longest relevant shadow interval, time waiting for contacts, and sensitivity to uncertain terrain or asset availability. The longest dark interval can matter more than the average illuminated fraction because the battery must bridge a continuous energy deficit. The illumination literature explicitly analyzes continuous illumination and shadow durations as well as averages, which is the research basis for retaining those temporal metrics. A favorable annual average must not be presented as a guarantee for the user's selected mission dates. ([P04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p04), [P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05))

Earth visibility needs the same care. The NASA polar-illumination product notes that its Earth-visibility map considers Earth visible when any part of the disk is unobscured, which can be optimistic for links to particular DSN stations. SOMA should show the Earth disk for intuition but calculate communications to actual configured station coordinates. A small visible part of Earth may not contain the selected station; that station can also be on Earth's far side or below its own local elevation limit. The useful comparison is therefore “this specific end-to-end schedule works,” not merely “Earth is visible more often.” ([D04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d04), [P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11))

## What passing validation should look like

Before using this model to rank scenarios, verify a synthetic flat horizon, a known ridge blocking one direction, separate panel and antenna heights, partial solar-disk visibility, a shadow interval that drains a battery, a full battery that spills excess charge, and a transmission that violates instantaneous power despite sufficient stored energy. Then compare an independently generated horizon or illumination sequence for a published location, with the same dates, heights, terrain, frames, and solar-visibility definition. Differences should be explained rather than hidden by copying a published percentage. The proposed deliverable is a validated implementation of stated assumptions, not a reproduction claim based only on a visually similar map.
