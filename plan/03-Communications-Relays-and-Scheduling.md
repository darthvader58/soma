# Part 3: Communications, Relays, and Scheduling

## Begin with the delivery task, not a predetermined chain

The network should be represented as a set of possible assets and time-dependent connections. A lander might communicate directly with a ground station, through a lunar relay, or through another explicitly configured route. The architecture should not begin with a rule that every dataset must pass through a lunar satellite and then an Earth-orbit satellite. Israel and colleagues describe LunaNet as an extensible architecture with different possible node arrangements, and the current interoperability specification explicitly covers both direct-with-Earth and lunar-relay capabilities. These sources support flexibility, not one mandatory topology. ([P09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p09), [D06](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d06))

For SOMA, the recommended baseline is direct-to-Earth plus one or a few preset lunar relays and a geographically distributed set of receiving stations. NASA's DSN provides a real example of ground complexes distributed around Earth, at Goldstone, Madrid, and Canberra. SOMA may use documented station locations or clearly labeled educational proxies, but visibility of a station is not permission to use its antenna. Real antenna performance, frequency compatibility, maintenance, competing missions, and allocated service must come from a separate profile and availability calendar. A ground-network preset should never imply that NASA has reserved those resources for the user. ([D10](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d10), [D09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d09))

Lunar relay operation is not merely a speculative idea. Zhang and colleagues' Queqiao-2 system paper reports its design, in-orbit testing, and relay support for Chang'e-6. It also describes the earlier Queqiao mission's Earth-Moon L2-region relay heritage. This is evidence that lunar-relay-to-ground architectures have been implemented and tested. It is not a public service guarantee, an open ephemeris feed, or proof that SOMA's chosen site and radio can use those spacecraft. In contrast, Brack and colleagues' Lunar Data Network paper is explicitly a preliminary constellation-design study. Its five-satellite elliptical frozen-orbit architecture is valuable design evidence, but its planned deployment statements should not be converted into a claim that every asset is currently operating. ([P07](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p07), [P08](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p08))

Earth-orbit relays should remain optional. Wang, Wang, and Wang compare a primary path through a lunar orbiter with a secondary path through an Earth-orbiting relay in an emulated cislunar network. These are alternative paths in their experiment, not proof that the two relays should always be chained together. Their testbed uses three Linux computers, a specified channel rate, selected delay and error settings, a one-megabyte file, and no introduced link disruptions. It supports conclusions about the tested protocol configurations under those conditions. It does not establish universal Earth-relay superiority, physical link closure, useful orbital coverage, or an operational service. SOMA should add an Earth-orbit relay only when its own input geometry, compatible terminals, service windows, and performance assumptions justify it. ([P10, Sections 5 and 8](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p10))

## Turn geometric opportunities into usable contacts

A geometric opportunity answers a narrow question: can a line between two endpoints avoid the relevant bodies and terrain? A usable contact answers a larger question: can these two configured systems actually exchange data during this interval? SOMA should keep those records separate. A candidate interval becomes usable only after geometry, terminal compatibility, pointing, service availability, radio-resource limits, and link-performance assumptions have been evaluated. The contact-plan literature represents a contact with a sender, receiver, start, stop, rate, and propagation-delay information. The physics needed to generate that contact is an additional layer, not something a graph-routing algorithm supplies. ([Moy et al., P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11), [D07](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d07))

SOMA should use one mandatory endpoint-time convention: every contact start and stop is expressed as a **source transmit window**. For a signal emitted at time t, derive its receiver-arrival time by adding the modeled one-way light time. Receiver service availability and terminal occupancy must be evaluated at those arrival epochs, with the eligible receiver intervals mapped back to the source-transmit timeline when constructing contacts. Use that same convention for capacity allocation, radio conflicts, receive-side energy, buffer availability, and the final-byte deadline. It is a SOMA data-contract choice, not a claim that every external contact format uses the same convention; importers must convert explicitly.

For lander-to-space links, check the local lunar terrain and the Moon's body. For relay-to-Earth links, check relevant Moon and Earth occultation and the actual receiving station's local elevation limit. For a finite-distance target, only terrain along the segment to that target can obstruct it; a mountain beyond a nearby target is not a blocker. A link to a particular station must use that station's position, not merely the Earth-center direction. The source and receiver should share a consistent coordinate system and time convention, and near-horizon opportunities should include a declared clearance margin rather than using an exact zero-crossing as an operational guarantee. ([P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [P05](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p05), [D04](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d04))

The initial implementation can use same-epoch geometric line-of-sight tests as an explicitly labeled approximation, while adding propagation delay to delivery times. Before claiming accuracy at tight boundaries, compare this approximation with transmission-light-time-corrected geometry for representative links and incorporate the measured discrepancy into the event margin or improve the model. A receiver moves during signal travel, and a lunar terrain frame also changes orientation. SPICE supports the underlying geometry and correction conventions, but the planner must choose and record them consistently. “We used SPICE” is not a sufficient description of the calculation. ([P02](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p02), [D03](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d03))

Radio compatibility should be an explicit gate. A visible receiver in the wrong band is not reachable; a relay's lander-facing and Earth-facing terminals may be different systems. Antenna boresight, allowed steering, acquisition time, Sun-avoidance limits where applicable, and simultaneous-use restrictions belong in the terminal profile. If attitude or gimbal behavior is simplified to ideal pointing, the result must carry that assumption. Surface-to-surface diffraction, multipath, and a full terrain-aware electromagnetic propagation model are outside the initial scope. The first release should use conservative line-of-sight clearance and refuse to interpret a grazing ray as a validated RF channel. ([P07](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p07), [P15](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p15), [D09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d09))

## Estimate link performance with a documented radio model

The free-space starting point is the Friis transmission relation. In its common gain-based form, received power equals transmitted RF power multiplied by transmit gain, receive gain, and the square of wavelength divided by four pi times distance, with additional losses accounted for separately. Equivalently, free-space path loss in decibels is 20 times the base-10 logarithm of four pi times distance divided by wavelength. This is a research-established far-field free-space relation, not a model of an obstructed lunar surface path. The original paper explicitly discusses its limitations. A link budget adds pointing, polarization, hardware, and propagation losses appropriate to the scenario. Earth-side atmospheric effects should be considered where relevant, rather than importing an atmosphere onto the lunar surface. ([Friis, P15](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p15), [D09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d09))

To choose a data rate, the model needs receiver noise performance and a declared modulation/coding mode with a required signal-quality threshold. One consistent convention is to compute carrier-to-noise-density ratio, subtract 10 times the logarithm of the information bit rate to obtain energy-per-bit to noise-density ratio, and compare that quantity with the mode's requirement plus an engineering margin. The units and bit-rate convention must match the threshold. Alternatively, a verified mode table may directly specify required carrier-to-noise-density ratios. In either case, coding and protocol overhead must be handled once, not subtracted twice. The result should distinguish gross channel rate from payload goodput, which is the rate of useful file data that the scheduler can allocate. ([D09](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d09); [protocol-goodput distinctions, P10](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p10))

A conservative first release should use a small set of discrete radio modes rather than pretend it can continuously choose any rate. Each mode should state its payload-rate convention, required threshold, antenna assumptions, and incremental electrical demand. If a link budget cannot be assembled because required hardware inputs are missing, the output should remain a geometric opportunity or an assumed-rate simulation, not a physically assessed contact. Preset rates can be useful for teaching and software tests, but their labels must say “assumed net rate.” A deterministic predicted rate is also not a guarantee against equipment failures or unmodeled channel conditions. The differences between geometry, assumed service, modeled link feasibility, and actual service need to survive into every export.

## Store-and-forward changes the meaning of a route

A continuous simultaneous path is not always required. A lander can send data to a relay during one interval, the relay can retain it, and the relay can forward it during a later Earth contact. NASA's DTN description and the contact-routing literature establish this store-and-forward model. A bent-pipe relay, by contrast, requires the appropriate overlapping links and does not automatically provide that waiting capability. SOMA must make relay behavior a capability in the profile. It should never attach unlimited storage to an arbitrary spacecraft just because the graph representation makes that convenient. ([P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11), [P12](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p12), [D07](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d07))

Time and volume both matter. If a net payload rate is constant, contact capacity is rate multiplied by usable duration. If rate changes, capacity is the integral of rate through the usable interval. Acquisition, teardown, guard time, unavailable service, and already allocated capacity reduce what is available. A large file cannot be declared deliverable merely because the first bit can follow a path. The last byte must traverse every required hop before the deadline, and the intermediate nodes must have enough buffer space. The CCSDS schedule-aware routing standard explicitly distinguishes earliest transmission, radiation latency, arrival, backlog, and route-volume limits. ([D07, Sections 3.2.4 and 3.2.6](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#d07), [P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11))

The first product should assume one dataset is ready at a known time. It may split that dataset into documented planning chunks so several contacts can carry it, while preserving causal forwarding: a chunk cannot leave a relay before it has been received. A logical Earth sink can count unique bytes received at any configured eligible ground station, but that is an accounting convention for delivery to the ground network, not a simulation of terrestrial delivery to a particular mission-control computer. Streaming data generation, competing traffic priorities, cross-mission reservations, and adaptive rerouting can remain later features. Existing or assumed competing allocations can initially appear as reductions in the available contact calendar.

## A research-backed scheduling formulation

A time-expanded graph represents the same node at different times. A transfer edge moves data between nodes, and a carry edge retains it at the same node until a later time. Li and colleagues explicitly formulate link capacity from rate and interval length and represent storage through bounded carry edges. Their model also includes transceiver and energy resources. Liu and colleagues formulate an Earth remote-sensing scheduling problem using temporal flows, finite data requirements, start/end constraints, storage limits, and binary equipment-use decisions, yielding a mixed-integer linear program. Those papers provide direct backing for the mathematical approach. They do not validate lunar link geometry or prove that their original energy assumptions fit a lunar lander. ([P12, Section 3.2](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p12), [P13, Sections 2.2 and 2.3](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p13))

SOMA should adapt that approach to a much smaller centralized problem. Build contacts from the geometry and radio models, divide the bounded horizon into event-aligned time bins, and allocate data subject to capacity and storage constraints. Use binary activity decisions where a terminal cannot serve two links at once or where a radio mode has an on/off electrical cost. Reserve receive activity at the proper arrival interval as well as transmit activity at the source. A conservative bin model can hold a transferred chunk until the bin ends and its light-time delay has elapsed before making it available for forwarding. That loses some efficiency but avoids instantaneous teleportation through a chain. The chosen time resolution and its conservatism must be reported and tested.

For the initial search, define a limited set of route families: lander directly to the Earth network, or lander through one selected lunar relay to that network. Multiple ground contacts may be used within a family. A separately enabled family may include an Earth-orbit relay, but it is not necessary for the baseline demo. Solving over a restricted set makes the result explainable and keeps the implementation bounded; it also means “no feasible plan” means none was found in those families and that time discretization. It is not a proof that every physically possible network would fail.

The default product policy should be explicit: first satisfy the deadline and all constraints, then prefer fewer relay hops, then earlier completion, with lander communications energy as a further tie-breaker. This implements a preference for a simple direct route when it is feasible without asserting that direct-to-Earth always minimizes energy or completion time. An optional earliest-delivery policy can prioritize completion time instead, but the selected policy must be recorded. These priorities are SOMA design choices, not conclusions of the cited papers. If a solver stops at a time limit, distinguish a feasible incumbent from a proven optimum, and distinguish “unknown” from a proven infeasible discretized model.

Battery constraints from Part 4 should participate in the optimization, including its exclusive charge/discharge rule and explicit start-to-end-of-bin energy update. Precompute generation and base demand on the chosen bins, integrate scheduled radio activity over them, and check the resulting battery state at every boundary. Refine bins or use conservative bounds where shorter variations could violate an instantaneous power or reserve limit; a positive end-of-bin balance alone does not prove that the battery survived the whole interval. For external relays whose full power system is unknown, use a stated service-availability assumption instead of inventing a battery model. For any node whose storage or simultaneous radio capability is unknown, a preset must explicitly choose a bounded assumption or mark the route unsupported. A plain shortest-path algorithm is useful for initial reachability and candidate generation, but it is not by itself sufficient once finite data, resource conflicts, and energy are enforced. Nor should this planner be described as a compliant implementation of CCSDS SABR simply because it uses contact-plan concepts. ([P11](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p11), [P12](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p12), [P13](06-Research-and-Source-Register.md#p13))

## A small worked example, explicitly not a mission result

Consider a synthetic software test in which 100 MiB is available at 08:50 UTC and must reach Earth by 10:05 UTC. This is 104,857,600 bytes, or 838,860,800 bits. Assume a direct contact runs from 08:50 to 09:10 at a net rate of 256,000 bits per second. Even with no overhead beyond that already included in the net rate, it can carry only 307,200,000 bits, so it cannot deliver the whole file. Assume the next direct opportunity begins after the deadline. Those times and rates are invented test inputs, not predictions for a lunar site or actual spacecraft.

Now assume a lander-to-relay contact from 09:00 to 09:10 at a net rate of 2,000,000 bits per second, and a relay-to-ground contact from 10:00 to 10:03 at 10,000,000 bits per second. The first hop needs approximately 419.43 seconds, or seven minutes, and the second approximately 83.89 seconds. With sufficient relay storage, compatible radios, adequate energy, and the stated contact assumptions, the file can wait on the relay and finish the second transmission around 10:01:24, before adding that hop's propagation and processing delay. The capacity arithmetic shows why waiting is part of a route. It does not establish that these contacts exist, that the radios close the links, or that a real lander can afford the transmissions.

The test becomes more valuable when one constraint is changed. Make the relay buffer too small, disable store-and-forward, force an incompatible terminal, overlap two uses of a half-duplex radio, or reduce the lander's reserve below what the transfer needs. The correct answer should then change for the specific reason introduced. The same example should produce a later completion when rate is reduced and should never deliver more unique bytes than were available at the source. These are proposed acceptance tests for the scheduler, not claimed measurements of SOMA performance.

## What the planner should return

A successful result should contain a route family, a sequence of transmission allocations, explicit storage intervals, the final-byte arrival time, the volume delivered, and the modeled resource histories. Every transmission should identify its underlying contact and radio mode, and every result should include assumptions and provenance. An unsuccessful result should identify a concrete limiting condition where it can be diagnosed, such as insufficient available volume before the deadline or an energy reserve violation. When several constraints interact, report that the bounded model is infeasible rather than pretending there is one proven sole cause. The scientific value lies in an auditable explanation of what the model permits, not in drawing a path regardless of whether the data can actually traverse it.
