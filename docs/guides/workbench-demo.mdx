Use the [live workbench](https://soma-atlas-two.vercel.app/) for this recording. Geometry and Data delivery calculate different quantities. The global geometry endpoint uses a reference sphere; the regional delivery endpoint separately evaluates numerical terrain, illumination, energy, contacts and scheduling under explicit assumptions.

Record the dark dashboard at 1920 × 1080, 100% zoom, with microphone narration and Cesium attribution visible. Rehearse once and wait for the initial calculation and Moon tiles to finish before recording. Keep this guide, the app and the [Python tutorial](/getting-started/live-research) in separate tabs. Use normal speaking speed and hold each important numerical state for several seconds. The actions and narration below target **nine minutes**, including fresh calculation time and deliberate inspection. Do not substitute captured results if a live calculation fails.

## 0:00–0:45 — State the research question

**Record:** Show the whole workbench: several selected locations, the Moon, an LRO track, UTC controls and the inspector. Keep playback paused.

**Say:**

“A lunar map is useful for finding a place. Planning an observation also requires asking what is visible from that place, at a particular time, and which data supports the answer.

This is SOMA. I can select several published lunar locations, add my own coordinates, change the date, and ask Python to calculate a new study. The browser displays those returned results rather than animating a preset answer. Today I will compare the Sun and Earth geometry at different locations, inspect a reconstructed spacecraft trajectory, and export a calculation that another researcher can reproduce.”

## 0:45–1:35 — Inspect multiple real references

**Record:** Inspect Apollo 11. Open its coordinate source in another tab, briefly show the NASA table, then return. Inspect Haworth and Tycho. Use **Global** and **South pole**. Hold the source notes and comparison table.

**Say:**

“The catalog includes six historical Apollo landing sites and nine named crater centers. These are sourced references, with different levels of coordinate information. NASA explicitly identifies the Apollo coordinates in the DE421 mean-Earth system. The crater records preserve their USGS source and precision notes.

A crater center is not automatically a suitable landing site. These markers also use a declared zero elevation on a reference sphere; they are not a claim of precise global registration to the rendered terrain. That distinction matters when a visual feature could otherwise look more certain than the underlying data.

The comparison uses the same UTC interval and sampling for every selected location. I can inspect the different Sun and Earth center elevations without assigning an unexplained best-site score.”

## 1:35–2:35 — Follow an actual archived spacecraft

**Record:** Click **Use LRO coverage**, wait for calculation, then **Inspect stored state** and **Follow spacecraft**. Click **Next stored sample** twice. Play at 600× or 3600×, then pause after at least ten seconds. Scrub to a different UTC epoch.

**Say:**

“This spacecraft is NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Its trajectory comes from a reconstructed SPICE product in the public PDS archive. It is not a synthetic ellipse and it is not a live position feed. The selected source covers March fifteenth through June fifteenth, 2026, so SOMA chooses a covered historical interval.

Here are the native Moon-fixed position and velocity values for a stored epoch. Advancing the sample changes those numerical states. Playback follows the supplied history, and I can stop at a time of interest.

Between stored epochs, the browser interpolates position only for smooth presentation. The numerical inspector identifies its stored sample. The application does not extrapolate this trajectory beyond its coverage, and it does not infer a communications relay service or an instrument footprint from the orbit alone.”

## 2:35–3:40 — Calculate a location that was not a preset

**Record:** Pause. Click **+ Custom site**. Enter latitude **21.1827**, east longitude **68.2714**, panel height **1.7 m**, antenna height **2 m**, and elevation **0 m**. Show the stale-result message, click **Run analysis**, then hold the new four-site comparison and study hash.

**Say:**

“I am now adding a user-defined study location: twenty-one point one eight two seven degrees north, sixty-eight point two seven one four degrees east. This is my declared input, not another catalog entry. I will give it a one point seven meter panel height and keep the antenna height separate.

Editing the input marks the previous calculation stale. That prevents an old plot from being presented as the answer to a new request. Running analysis sends the selected sites and UTC epochs to Python. The resulting hash identifies the normalized request, and the fourth row contains the new site's calculated geometry.

The elevations here are Sun and Earth center directions relative to a spherical local horizontal. Positive elevation does not establish terrain-clear access. The geometry result explicitly says that terrain shadows, finite solar-disk visibility, eclipses, power and usable contacts were not evaluated.”

## 3:40–4:35 — Make a controlled date comparison

**Record:** Save the calculated alternative. Change both date fields from **1 April** to **2 April 2026**, preserving 00:00–06:00 UTC and the same sampling/heights. Run again. Show the changed hash and elevation readouts, then the saved alternatives table. Do not claim it provides an automatic scientific ranking.

**Say:**

“A map point by itself does not change when I ask a different research question. Its geometry can. I am keeping the site set, elevations, equipment heights and sampling unchanged, while moving the six-hour interval forward by one day.

This is another calculation, with another request hash. The new values come from the selected ephemerides for those epochs. The saved alternative retains the earlier result and its input metadata, so I can distinguish the two studies. It does not manufacture a ranking or hide which conditions changed.

Coverage and numerical budgets are also explicit. Unsupported trajectory dates are rejected. A request that exceeds the sampled-epoch or response budget must be made smaller; scientific arrays are not silently cut down.”

## 4:35–7:35 — Plan actual file delivery in the model

**Record:** Choose **Data delivery**. Show its declared terrain coverage, 16 MiB task, November 24 08:00–14:00 UTC interval, initial battery and network assumptions. Disable **One lunar store-and-forward relay**, leaving **Direct to Earth**. Choose **Plan delivery** and hold the actual outcome and last-byte Earth arrival. Focus its selected transfer and choose **Earth network**.

Next change all four task epochs to November 30, retaining 08:00 readiness/start and 14:00 deadline/end. Choose **Use analysis interval as assumed service**, then calculate with direct alone. Read its actual limiting explanation. Enable the lunar relay and calculate again. Focus the lander-to-relay transfer, the relay wait and the later Earth transfer. Hold the battery and buffer history.

**Say:**

“Geometry alone does not answer whether a complete file reaches Earth. Here I declare the file size, readiness, deadline, finite storage, battery and a small network. This separate provider uses published numerical LOLA terrain and matched ephemerides within the region and dates shown here.

The radio rates, ideal pointing and station service are assumptions. These station locations are educational geographic proxies, not reserved DSN antennas. The optional relay uses a labeled synthetic lunar orbit. The LRO reconstruction I showed earlier does not supply relay service.

I will first ask for direct delivery on November twenty-fourth. Python prepares the contacts and power, solves the constrained schedule, and independently replays it. The number to inspect is the last byte arriving at Earth, together with the solver and replay status. A usable contact by itself does not deliver the dataset.

Now I move the task and its explicitly assumed service to November thirtieth, preserving the six-hour deadline rule, file size, location, hardware and initial battery. Direct delivery has to stand on the contacts calculated for that date. Its limiting explanation comes from the engine.

Enabling the lunar relay changes one declared route family. If the calculated schedule succeeds, the timeline shows the first hop, when the bytes become available, where they wait, and the later ground hop. The buffer and battery histories expose the resource consequences. If this run is unresolved or infeasible, that is the result to report.

The network camera uses the actual supplied positions. Earth is far from the Moon, so the Moon becomes small at this scale. The line represents a selected transmission interval; it is not a claim of an operational communications service or flight validation.”

The verified comparison used fresh Linux Vercel calculations with 16 MiB, the default explicit profiles and a six-hour interval. Independently replayed downloads confirmed direct delivery on November 24, no direct contact capacity on November 30, and relay delivery on November 30. Solver incumbents and precise arrival epochs may depend on declared discretization and solve budget; read the current output instead of narrating an unverified fixed timestamp. See [live delivery](/getting-started/live-delivery).

## 7:35–8:30 — Export and reproduce

**Record:** In Data delivery choose **Python run package**. Show its scenario, result, manifest and schedule CSV, then the independent replay command in the documentation. Return to Geometry and show **Result JSON**, including `request`, `study_sha256`, `provenance`, `kernels`, `spacecraft_sources` and `limitations`. Open **Python reference** and show the module references and capability matrix.

**Say:**

“The delivery download preserves the Python run package, with its scenario, contacts, schedule, resources, replay inputs and source manifest. The browser verifies the ZIP checksum rather than rebuilding it from the chart. The independent verifier can replay the package outside this website.

The geometry export also contains the exact scientific response, including the normalized request, sampled arrays, software versions and source checksums. Someone else can verify the hash and rerun that request with the same pinned sources.

The documentation covers the implemented geometry, trajectory, terrain, illumination, power, communications, scheduling and export modules. It gives their arguments, units, examples and limitations. The capability matrix also identifies unbuilt instrument, coverage and trade-space workflows; SOMA does not claim full TATC feature parity.

The public application and its synchronous Python API both run on Vercel. Durable background jobs are not advertised on this deployment.”

## 8:30–9:00 — Finish with the supported contribution

**Record:** Return to the app. Select **Global**, briefly play the reconstructed history, then pause with the inspector and comparison visible.

**Say:**

“SOMA connects lunar geometry with an auditable data-delivery question. I can calculate new site and time studies, inspect spacecraft states, declare a bounded network and ask when a complete dataset reaches Earth within its resource constraints.

The user can inspect what was calculated, which inputs are published or assumed, and what remains unresolved. Those limits travel with the exported result, so another researcher can review the model and reproduce the calculation.”
